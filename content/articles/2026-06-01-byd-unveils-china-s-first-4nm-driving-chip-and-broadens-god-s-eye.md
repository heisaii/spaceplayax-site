# BYD unveils China’s first 4nm driving chip and broadens God’s Eye

BYD has introduced the Xuanji A3, which it describes as China’s first automotive-grade 4nm chip for self-driving cars, while also widening the reach of its God’s Eye driver-assistance system to mass-market electric vehicles. The announcement places the company at the center of two fast-moving parts of the auto industry at once: in-house semiconductor design and the push to bring advanced driver-assistance features to lower-priced models.

According to the source, the Xuanji A3 delivers 700 TOPS per chip, a measure of compute performance used in artificial intelligence and driving systems. In practical terms, that level of processing is aimed at the kinds of workloads modern assisted-driving systems require, including sensing, decision-making and real-time responses from vehicle software. The fact that BYD is presenting the chip as automotive-grade and built on a 4nm process underscores the company’s ambition to compete in a segment where performance, efficiency and integration increasingly matter.

The move comes as BYD expands God’s Eye beyond premium or showcase vehicles and into mass-market EVs, including the Seagull, which the source identifies as a $10,300 model. That detail is significant because it suggests BYD is not treating advanced driver assistance as a feature reserved only for expensive vehicles. Instead, the company appears to be making it part of a broader product strategy that reaches deeper into its mainstream lineup.

Driver-assistance systems have become one of the most visible areas of competition in the electric-vehicle market. Automakers are under pressure to offer features that improve convenience, confidence and perceived technological sophistication, even in lower-cost vehicles. At the same time, the software and hardware behind such systems can be expensive and complex to develop. BYD’s combination of a homegrown driving chip and a wider rollout of God’s Eye indicates an effort to control more of that stack internally.

That approach could matter for several reasons. First, integrating chip design with vehicle software may give BYD more flexibility in how it deploys features across its portfolio. Second, building a chip specifically for driving applications can help align hardware performance with the needs of the company’s assistance system. Third, rolling these capabilities into a mass-market EV suggests BYD believes advanced assistance can be delivered at scale rather than kept as a niche premium offering.

The timing of the announcement also stands out. The source notes that BYD’s sales have fallen for an eighth straight month. In that context, the company’s emphasis on new technology looks as much like a product and brand statement as a technical one. When sales are under pressure, automakers often lean harder on innovation, feature differentiation and platform upgrades to reinforce their market position. BYD’s latest announcements fit that pattern, presenting the company as both a hardware innovator and a volume manufacturer.

The Xuanji A3 and the expansion of God’s Eye also reflect a wider industry trend: the growing convergence of vehicle manufacturing, semiconductor design and software-defined mobility. Cars are no longer judged only by range or styling. Increasingly, buyers and investors look at the intelligence of the vehicle itself, especially the quality of its assistance systems and the efficiency of the computing platforms that run them. In that sense, BYD’s new chip is not just a component launch; it is part of a broader effort to shape how its vehicles are perceived in a competitive EV market.

Even so, the source leaves open important questions about deployment, pricing impact and the exact scope of features available on different models. What is clear from the announcement is that BYD is pushing advanced driver-assistance technology further down-market while simultaneously building the specialized silicon intended to support it. That combination positions the company to control more of the technological foundation of its electric vehicles and to bring higher-end driving capabilities to models aimed at mainstream buyers.
