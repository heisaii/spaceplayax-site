# Vance Warns Air Force Cadets About AI’s Role in Warfare

Vice President JD Vance warned graduating Air Force cadets on Thursday about the dangers of artificial intelligence in warfare, urging them to embrace advancing technology without becoming dependent on it. His remarks centered on a central tension now confronting modern militaries: how to use AI to strengthen capability while preserving human judgment in decisions that can have life-or-death consequences.

Speaking to the new graduates, Vance encouraged them to use emerging tools to improve their effectiveness, but he cautioned against surrendering responsibility to those tools. The message reflected a broader concern that, while artificial intelligence can enhance analysis, speed, and operational efficiency, it cannot replace the judgment, discipline, and accountability expected of military officers.

The warning lands at a moment when artificial intelligence is moving quickly into discussions about defense, security, and warfare. Military institutions across the world are weighing how to apply automated systems, machine learning, and data-driven decision support without allowing technology to determine outcomes on its own. In that context, Vance’s remarks underscored the idea that military strength depends not only on having better tools, but also on knowing where those tools should stop.

The vice president’s comments also highlight the special responsibilities of Air Force officers as they begin their careers. Cadets entering service today will do so in an environment where technological change is accelerating and where future conflicts may be shaped by faster information flows, more complex sensing systems, and increasingly sophisticated software. That reality makes adaptation essential, but it also raises the stakes for human oversight.

Vance’s warning suggests that artificial intelligence should be treated as an aid rather than an authority. In warfare, decisions are not purely technical. They involve judgment, command responsibility, and the moral burden of using force. Even when technology can process data more quickly than people can, it does not shoulder accountability for the consequences. That distinction appeared to sit at the heart of his message to the graduates.

The remarks also fit a longstanding military principle: new technology can expand capability, but it can also create new vulnerabilities if it is adopted uncritically. In the case of artificial intelligence, those vulnerabilities may include overreliance on automated recommendations, reduced human awareness, or a false sense of certainty when systems appear to offer precision. Vance’s warning did not reject progress. Instead, it framed caution as part of professionalism.

For graduating cadets, the speech served as both encouragement and admonition. They were told to take advantage of advancing technology in order to become more effective officers, but they were also reminded that their duty is to command technology, not be commanded by it. That distinction may prove especially important as AI continues to influence planning, logistics, intelligence work, and other areas that support military operations.

The message is likely to resonate beyond the ceremony itself. As AI becomes more deeply embedded in defense conversations, questions about control, responsibility, and the limits of automation are likely to intensify. Vance’s comments to the Air Force cadets placed those questions squarely in the language of leadership and service, emphasizing that technological advantage should strengthen human decision-making rather than replace it. In that sense, the warning was not only about artificial intelligence. It was also about the enduring demands of command in an age of rapid change.
